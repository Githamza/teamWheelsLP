## 1. Why commuting is the blind spot in most ESG strategies {#blind-spot}

> **Key takeaway** — For a service-sector company, employee commuting often represents **20 to 40% of the total carbon footprint**. Yet it remains one of the least-managed lines in corporate ESG strategies, for lack of reliable data and a simple lever to act on.

Most ESG programs concentrate on direct emissions (Scope 1) and energy (Scope 2): fleet, buildings, electricity. These are visible, controllable categories. But the moment you cross into **Scope 3** — indirect emissions — the daily journeys of your workforce become one of the largest contributors… and the hardest to reduce.

Why this blind spot? Because the commute is the result of thousands of individual choices. A sustainability director cannot directly "decarbonize" an employee's personal car. What they *can* do is **organize the sharing of those journeys** — which is exactly what corporate carpooling enables.

Carpooling is one of the rare ESG levers that is simultaneously:

- **Environmental** — every shared trip takes a vehicle off the road;
- **Social** — it directly improves employees' purchasing power;
- **Economic** — it costs a fraction of other levers (fleet electrification, carbon offsets);
- **Measurable** — every shared mile is traceable and auditable.

## 2. Carpooling and Scope 3: the most accessible carbon lever {#scope-3}

Under the **GHG Protocol Corporate Value Chain Standard**, employee commuting falls under **Scope 3 Category 7**. In practice, every employee who moves from solo driving to carpooling divides the footprint of their trip by the number of people in the vehicle.

### A simple, auditable calculation

Take an average 20-mile commute, 4 days a week:

| Scenario | Vehicles / day | Annual CO₂ per employee |
|---|---|---|
| Solo commute | 1 | ~ 1.6 tonnes |
| Carpool of 2 | 0.5 | ~ 0.8 tonnes |
| Carpool of 3 | 0.33 | ~ 0.55 tonnes |

For a 200-person workforce where 40% adopt two-person carpooling, the company avoids **dozens of tonnes of CO₂ per year** — a result that is directly deductible from the carbon footprint, provided you **measure the miles actually shared**.

> **The key is the data.** A credible ESG strategy does not merely estimate. It proves. This is where most carpooling programs fail: without a tracking tool, it is impossible to document the CO₂ avoided. An integrated platform like TeamWheels automatically records every shared trip, its mileage, and the corresponding CO₂ — **audit-ready** data for your reporting. ## 3. CSRD, SB 253 & the disclosure landscape {#disclosure}

Regulatory pressure is no longer a matter of goodwill: it is **quantified and mandatory** for a growing number of companies on both sides of the Atlantic.

### The CSRD (Europe)

The **Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD)**, in force for large EU companies since 2024 and progressively extended to listed SMEs, requires companies to quantify and publish **Scope 3** emissions under the **ESRS E1** climate standard. Employee commuting is explicitly in scope.

But CSRD does not stop at carbon. The social standard **ESRS S1** expects indicators on workforce conditions — which includes access to affordable mobility. A documented carpooling program **answers both requirements at once**: environmental (E1) and social (S1).

### SB 253 and US disclosure (United States)

California's **SB 253** (Climate Corporate Data Accountability Act) requires large companies doing business in California to disclose Scope 3 emissions starting in 2027 — and explicitly lists employee commuting as a category. With the **SBTi** tightening its Net-Zero Standard and investors demanding granular ESG data via **CDP**, commuting emissions have moved from afterthought to boardroom priority across US enterprises.

> **The takeaway** — Whether your reporting obligation is CSRD, SB 253, CDP, or voluntary SBTi alignment, they all converge on the same need: **defensible, granular data on employee commuting**. Carpooling is the rare lever that both *reduces* that line and *generates the evidence* to report it.

## 4. The three ESG pillars carpooling serves {#three-pillars}

The strength of carpooling is that it acts on **all three ESG dimensions** at the same time — Environmental, Social, and Governance.

### 🌍 Environmental

- Direct, measurable reduction of **Scope 3 Category 7** emissions;
- Less congestion and air pollution around your sites;
- Reduced parking demand — an often-underestimated land-use and carbon issue.

### 🤝 Social

- **Purchasing power**: an employee carpooling 4 of 5 days on a 20-mile commute saves **$1,000–$2,000 per year**;
- **Connection**: shared trips build relationships across teams and sites;
- **Mobility inclusion**: carpooling offers a solution to employees without a car or far from transit. It is a strong **wellbeing**, **DEI**, and **employer-brand** argument.
